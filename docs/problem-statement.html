Problem statement · Draft

Counted, Not Tracked

Australia counts its domestic homicides well. Nothing counts whether anything was ever done about why they happened.

DV Death Review Recommendation Tracker 4 November 2026

The deaths are counted. The recommendations are not.

A national, per-state, consistently defined count of domestic homicide already exists and is published every year by the Australian Institute of Criminology. It covers all eight jurisdictions, including the two that have no death review body at all. This project cites it rather than rebuilding it. What exists nowhere is a register of what the review bodies recommended after those deaths, and whether governments did it.

01 How many people die

National Homicide Monitoring Program · 1 July 2024 – 30 June 2025

279 homicide victims in Australia
90 domestic homicide incidents, 34% of all homicide incidents
46 intimate partner homicide deaths, down 16 on the year

A third of the people killed in Australia are killed by family. The intimate partner figure fell by 16 deaths on the previous year, the third-lowest rate recorded. The national domestic homicide rate was 0.33 per 100,000.

Is there a running total

There is no official running count, and this is the one real gap in the death data. Homicide in Australia 2024–25 was published in February 2026 and its data stops at 30 June 2025. So today the most recent official national count covers a year that ended sixteen months ago, and the next release is not due until around February 2027. Nobody official can tell you how many women were killed last month.

Live tallies exist outside government, such as the volunteer-maintained Counting Dead Women. None has been verified here and this document quotes no figure from them. They count a different population (all violent deaths of women, not domestic homicide specifically) and they are not official. Using one is a trade-off, and it is left as an open question below rather than assumed.

02 Why the states can’t supply a national total

Each review body publishes on its own definition, unit and period

What each jurisdiction's review body publishes

These cannot be added together or compared. The periods differ by a factor of nearly four, the units are three different things, the definitions differ (DV-context vs DFV-related vs FDV), and half the country is absent. Anyone presenting a national figure built by summing these is presenting an artefact.

NSW's 550 is 30% of the 1,832 homicides over the same 22 years, and breaks down as 308 intimate partner, 96 filicide, 91 relative/kin and 55 other. Its own chapters quote different denominators for the same groups, because each table uses the subset for which that variable was known. Not an error, but it means a number must always be quoted with its table.

They do cross-check against the national series

NSW's 550 over 22 years is about 25 deaths a year, against the AIC's 23 and 26 incidents for NSW. Queensland's 134 deaths over 6 years is about 22, against 22 and 15. Western Australia's 237 over 13 years is about 18, against 14 and 16.

Not a reconciliation Incidents are not deceased persons, so these figures are not expected to match and have not been made to. The point is weaker and still useful: two independent data systems land in the same place in three jurisdictions, which is enough to trust the AIC series for a public-facing counter.

Two findings in the gaps

South Australia has stopped reporting. Across the seventeen SA State Coroner annual reports collected, the word “homicide” appears zero times in every report from 2018–19 onward, while “coroner” appears 76 to 129 times in those same files. So this is the reports' content, not a failed text extraction. The last SA report with substantial DV content was 2017–18.

Limit of this check It covers the State Coroner's annual reports, the only SA series collected. SA publishes individual inquest findings separately and those were not examined. The claim is that SA's annual reporting carries no DV death count, not that SA reviews nothing.

Two jurisdictions review nothing, and still bury people. Tasmania is the only state or territory with no domestic violence death review team; family-violence deaths are examined only by a coroner and those investigations are often not released publicly. A Coroners Act review was underway as of July 2026. Legislation in 2021 established an ACT Family Violence Death Review and the formal process is still being developed, so the ACT has a body but no published recommendation register. Victoria's Family Violence Reform Implementation Monitor tracked all 227 Royal Commission recommendations annually, then closed on 31 December 2022; monitoring moved inside the Department of Premier and Cabinet and is no longer independent or annual.

The AIC still counts these deaths: Tasmania 1 and 3, the ACT 0 and 3 across the two most recent years. Small numbers, and nobody is asking why any of them happened.

Source Jurisdiction status is taken from the AIJA National Domestic and Family Violence Bench Book, death review, last updated August 2025.

The count of 122 was reconciled against the Team's own published total, arrived at independently from five separate report chapters. Three of the 122 are split into lettered sub-parts, giving 125 rows. The status distribution is this:

Six of the 125 now have no usable status. Fifty had updates written as free prose rather than a status field. Each was read against its source and given a status with the government quote that justifies it. They were not keyword-matched: searching the prose produced a false “implemented” on a recommendation whose own update says “no formal response has been received”. Every row records which basis its status came from.

Of the nine refused or never started, seven sit with courts and justice. Three are “pending identification of resources”, with start dates that have since passed. The 2021–23 NSW report publishes no recommendations and no status chapter at all, so the 2019–21 and 2021–23 rounds have never been reported on.

The finding that matters most

Two recommendations were refused, and then quietly done anyway.

“The NSW Government is committed to the prevention of violence against women and children, however does not intend to become a member of Our Watch.”

Whole of government response, 2018. Recommendation 1.1

“Complete. In July 2019, the NSW Government joined Our Watch.”

Whole of government update, 2019. The same recommendation

That is published, government-sourced proof that “not supported” is a position, not an outcome. It is the answer to anyone who says writing to a minister changes nothing.

04 The path to action

Three steps

  1. The number, for their state. Ninety domestic homicides last year; twenty-three in NSW. A third of everyone killed in Australia, killed by family. Sourced to the AIC, shown per state, with the eight-fold rate spread visible rather than averaged away.
  2. These deaths were reviewed, and recommendations were written. Here are the recommendations for your state, what the government said about each one, and what it then did, or did not do. Refused and stalled items surface first, each linked to the page of the PDF it came from, so every claim is checkable.
  3. One click to act. A prefabricated email to the named minister about a specific stalled recommendation, quoting the recommendation and the government's own words about it. Already built for WA; NSW now has the recommendation data and needs the minister mapping.

Each email names one recommendation, the minister responsible for it, and the status the government itself recorded: “Recommendation 18 of the 2017–19 report, directed at your Attorney General, has been not started, pending identification of resources, since 2021.”

What this is not

  • Not a death counter. The AIC does that better than this project could. The site cites and links it.
  • Not victim-level detail. Nothing beyond what the source reports already published.
  • Not a judge scorecard. That was the original idea. Judge-level sentencing data is not published in Australia, and the idea carried real defamation exposure.

05 Open questions

These need a decision, not an assumption

  1. Live counter, or official only? An official-only counter is accurate and sixteen months stale. A volunteer tally is current, unofficial, and counts a different population. One option: lead with the AIC figure and show “and X since the last official count” from a clearly attributed secondary source. That source has to be verified first.
  2. All eight jurisdictions, or only the four with recommendations? Covering all eight makes “two jurisdictions review nothing” a visible finding rather than a footnote, at the cost of several states having a death count and an empty recommendations table.
  3. Spot-check the fifty coded statuses. All fifty have been read and coded. Thirty-four turned on a judgement call, most often an agency offering activity that already existed in place of what was asked. Those thirty-four want a second reader before the figures are quoted publicly.
  4. Queensland is not extracted. It is the one Tier 1 jurisdiction still showing headline numbers only. Its board publishes the strongest aggregate in the country, a cumulative table updated every year, so Queensland is the smallest remaining gap.
  5. Four repeats found. Whether that is all of them is unknown. A recommendation re-made years later is the clearest evidence of inaction. Four are confirmed, including one where the South Australian Coroner re-made an earlier recommendation word for word and said so. Candidates came from text similarity and the reports' own cross-references, so a repeat worded differently with nothing citing it would have been missed.
